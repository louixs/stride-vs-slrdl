In the RDL the working leg is always the one on the ground. Its hamstring and glute lengthen as you lower and shorten as you stand. It is slow, so it probably trains the muscle itself with little reflex or tendon-spring contribution (an inference, not a measurement).

Takeaways

  1. The SL-RDL loads the hamstrings and glutes at long muscle length, longer than at any point in the running stride. Whether that carries over to running at marathon pace is untested. Related exercises lengthen biceps femoris fascicles and raise EMG. The larger hip-extensor demand at 4 m/s comes at landing, in early stance. The lift is a strength exercise with its own tempo.
  2. Step rate is one lever on landing position. In 45 recreational runners at a self-selected 2.9 m/s on a treadmill, a 10% higher step rate brought the heel 2.2 cm closer to the centre of mass at contact (9.2 to 7.0 cm) and lowered peak hip flexion in the loading response from 26.7° to 23.6°. A 5% rise gave 7.8 cm and 25.3°, and the hip change was not significant.
  3. At marathon pace the calves do most of the propulsion and late-stance support, so soleus and gastrocnemius are a reasonable capacity target, though no trial shows calf training speeds a marathon.
  4. In the drawing the rear leg is held in line with the trunk (about 0°) as a counterweight; that is a convention, not a measurement. The lift does not practise toe-off, which needs about 10° of hip extension at marathon pace.
  5. Reflexes don't take over. Your brain switches the muscles on just before landing. A short-latency stretch reflex appears in the calf about 30 to 45 ms after contact, and the Achilles returns stored energy. The muscle still has to hold the tendon tight for the spring to work. How much force the reflex adds is still debated.

Where the force comes from: calf and quadriceps muscles switch on roughly 85 to 100 ms before contact, as a feedforward plan (Müller et al. 2015, running over drops; Avela et al. 1996, drop jumps). The stretch reflex shows up in the calf about 35 to 45 ms after contact (Dietz et al. 1979, as summarised in a thesis; original not checked), and its force contribution is debated (Melvill Jones and Watt 1971; Grillner 1972; Nicol and Komi 1999; Hof 2003). The Achilles tendon and foot arch store energy through roughly the first half of stance, after a brief recoil in the first 70 ms or so, and return it in the second half (Ker et al. 1987 estimated about 35 J for the Achilles tendon and 17 J for the arch per step for a 70 kg runner at 4.5 m/s, as quoted in secondary sources; later Achilles estimates range from about 10 to 70 J per stride across runners and methods). Soleus fibres change length relatively little while the tendon stretches (about 9 mm in ultrasound at 2 to 5 m/s); gastrocnemius fibres change more (about 13 mm) (Lai et al.). Quads, calves and hip muscles by phase: Hamner et al. 2010; Dorn et al. 2012; Chumanov et al. 2011. Lane windows also draw on EMG papers read as abstracts only (Gazendam and Hof 2007; Montgomery et al. 1994; Moran and Mann, ISBS). Hip-extension torque is from Schache et al. 2011 (8 runners, overground) and hamstring ultrasound from Mao et al. 2024 (7 sprinters). Swing knee motion is shaped by both momentum and muscles (Piazza and Delp 1996, walking). A pause between lowering and lifting reduces the benefit of the stretch-shorten cycle (Wilson et al. 1991, cited from a secondary source; not tested in a hip hinge). Lane windows are rounded from these sources and the stride timing on this page. The windows for glutes, hamstrings, hip flexors and the tendon halves are the page's own placements, so treat all windows as approximate.

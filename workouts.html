Running biomechanics / workout map

Running Workout Map

Slow lifts build capacity: force, tendon stiffness and fatigue tolerance late in a long run. Only running itself, with feedback, has controlled evidence of changing how you run. Weekly volume and training pace are the strongest observed predictors of marathon time, and strength work cannot stand in for them. Each of the ten workouts below is matched to one stride job, graded for evidence and placed in the season.

    02

    The ladder

    Slow strength, then isometrics, then slow drills, then fast elastic work, then running itself. It is a sensible load-and-speed progression. As a claim that each rung rewires the next, it is untested.

    Capacity flows upward

    Heavy work builds force, stiffness and fatigue tolerance that a runner can then spend at race pace. Controlled evidence exists for heavy strength (rung 1: economy and durability) and for tendon stiffness with high-intensity isometrics (rung 2: stiffness, with economy not significant). Fast elastic work (rung 4) has moderate support, mostly at 12 km/h or slower. Slow drills (rung 3) have no controlled evidence.

    Pattern change has evidence only at rung 5

    The retraining trials that worked delivered feedback during actual running, faded it over later sessions and reported retention afterwards. Slow drills are cheap scaffolding for awareness, with every position set to race-pace values and checked on video.

          05

          Eight self-tests

          Low-fatigue checks that tell him when to progress, hold or back off. Use the same time of day, shoes and warm-up. Trust within-person trends and left-right differences over absolute norms. His left side carries the tibial and outer-hip history.

          Economy gains of 2–5% cannot be measured outside a lab, and soreness is not a benchmark. The only direct late-run signal available to him is the drift test and the film. Race times will not do this job: volume and pacing dominate them.
